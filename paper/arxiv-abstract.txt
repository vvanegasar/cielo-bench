An agent that accepts a false claim when it cannot check it may simply not know. An agent that accepts the claim after its own tool returned evidence against it is agreeing with the user, not with the facts. cielo-bench is an open benchmark that separates the two cases. Each item is one question asked with a true and a false user premise, at three levels of user pressure, with and without a tool that can check it, and with that tool's data source failing. There are two domains: astronomy, with ground truth from NASA/JPL Horizons ephemerides for future dates (144 premise pairs, 18 templates), and synthetic company data queried with read-only SQL (126 pairs, 7 templates). Scoring is deterministic and uses no LLM judge. The headline metric, agree-after-check, is the share of false-premise trials in which the final verdict still agrees with the user after the tool returned contrary evidence. The instrument is validated with simulated agents, and first runs on four models from one provider are reported. With the tool, every model checked on every astronomy trial, and a model kept agreeing after a contradicting check in 2 of 1,080 false-premise trials; both cases come from one ambiguous template. The checks were easy and the false premises were far from the truth, so at this difficulty the benchmark cannot rank these models or assess pressure effects. Without the tool, in astronomy, the two models tested erred in both directions; on the fictional company data, Haiku abstained. When the source failed, they abstained without giving a value in roughly seven of ten astronomy trials; otherwise they gave a verdict or a value anyway, often an estimate from general knowledge. Trials of one item are not independent, so I report distinct pairs and templates, and sign tests over them, next to the main paired tests. Code, golden sets and sanitized transcripts are public.
